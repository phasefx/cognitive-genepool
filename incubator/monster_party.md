This project was an experiment in following a few assertions to their logical conclusions. 
It is a thinking artifact, not a toolkit. It has not been tested. It is not intended to be used.

If you're here to fix your life, your team, or your brain: **this is not the resource you need.**

### Monster Parties

#### Symbiotic Pathologies & Distributed Defense

*"You don't fight an addiction. You fight a party that's been running this dungeon longer than you've been awake."*

---

#### Why This Chapter Exists

The Monster Manual, until now, has described monsters as if they hunt alone.

They don't.

Lived failure—addiction, burnout, organizational rot, abusive dynamics, ideological capture—almost never presents as a single pattern. It presents as **coordinated opposition**. Multiple monsters, differentiated roles, covering each other's weaknesses.

The Player's Handbook already established that the self is a party—different cognitive modes that must coordinate. This chapter establishes the symmetry:

> **If the Player is a collective, the Pathology is almost certainly a coalition.**

Without this framing, you get predictable failures:

- You "defeat" one monster and the system compensates
- Interventions that should work don't
- Progress on one front worsens symptoms on another
- Helpers conclude "lack of will" or "bad faith" when the system is actually *responding strategically*

That's what happens when you fight a **party** with single-target spells.

---

#### Quick Diagnostic: The Party ID Card

When you suspect a Monster Party, map these:

| Question | What you're identifying |
|----------|------------------------|
| What behavior repeats despite consequences? | **DPS** |
| What makes alternatives invisible or impossible? | **Tank** |
| What story explains why this is fine? | **Healer** |
| What accumulating damage limits future options? | **Controller** |
| What anticipates and blocks intervention? | **Scout** (if present) |
| What narratives/rituals/cues coordinate them? | **Comms** |
| What happens when you attack one role? | **Compensation pattern** |
| What's your realistic intervention capacity? | **Action economy** |

If you can fill in most of this card, you're looking at a party, not a solo monster.

---

#### Parties vs. Chimeras

The MM already has a section on Chimeras—monsters that are fusions of two patterns. This is different.

| | Chimera | Monster Party |
|---|---|---|
| **Structure** | One entity, hybrid pattern | Multiple distinct entities, coordinated |
| **Example** | Captured Narrator + Metric Maximizer fused | Captured Narrator *defending* Metric Maximizer's territory |
| **Failure mode** | Pattern confusion | Role compensation |
| **Intervention** | Disentangle the fusion | Split the party |

A Chimera is a werewolf. A Monster Party is a wolf pack.

#### Two Ways to See This

There are two valid lenses for understanding Monster Parties. Both are true. Both are useful. They serve different purposes.

##### The Combat Lens (Tactical)

**Frame:** Two parties in opposition. Your healthy party vs. the Monster Party that has invaded or formed.

**Monsters are:** Adversaries to be defeated.

**Strategy:** Party wipe. Silence the Healer, Banish the Tank, Nuke the DPS.

**Core question:** *How do I defeat this?*

**Useful for:**
- Intervention planning
- Explaining why resistance is strategic, not moral failure
- Tactical thinking when you need to *fight*
- Working with external helpers who need clear targets

**Danger:** Can create internal civil war. Can frame parts of self as enemy. Can miss why the configuration exists.

---

##### The Integration Lens (Structural)

**Frame:** One party in pathological configuration. The Monster Party isn't an invading force—it's *your* party with certain members stuck in roles they won't relinquish.

**Monsters are:** Party members who seized control under load and won't rotate out.

**Strategy:** Role renegotiation. Spotlight sharing. Rebalancing. Meeting the underlying need differently.

**Terminus:** **Dissolution** — roles rotate, needs get met differently, monsters lose their jobs. The party doesn't die; it transforms.

**Core question:** *What need is this configuration trying to meet, and how can it be met another way?*

**Useful for:**
- Self-compassion
- Understanding *why* the configuration formed
- Long-term healing and integration
- Preventing the same party from reforming later

**Danger:** Can become excuse for inaction. Can over-empathize with patterns that are actively destructive. Can delay necessary intervention.

---

##### How They Relate

The Combat Lens and Integration Lens are not opposites. They're **zoom levels**.

Combat Lens is useful when you're *in* the crisis—when you need to break a loop, interrupt a pattern, create discontinuity. You can't integrate with something that's actively destroying you.

Integration Lens is useful *after* the acute phase—when you're trying to understand what happened, prevent recurrence, and find sustainable configurations. You can't fight forever.

**Typical sequence:**
1. Combat Lens to break the acute pattern
2. Integration Lens to understand and restructure
3. Maintenance awareness to catch early recruitment

Many interventions fail because they use the wrong lens for the phase:
- Integration Lens during crisis → "I just need to understand why I drink" (while still drinking)
- Combat Lens during recovery → "I must destroy this part of myself" (creates new pathology)

#### The Four Roles

**[Combat Lens]**

*When viewing the Monster Party as opposition, these are the functional roles that coordinate against you.*

Monster Parties tend to organize around four functional roles. Not every party has all four; some monsters can fill multiple roles; the roles describe *function*, not identity.

##### The Tank (Territory Holder)

**Function:** Absorbs damage. Blocks exits. Makes alternatives invisible or impossible.

**Typical Monsters:** Ossified Scaffold, Cirrhotic Liver (late-stage)

**How it protects the party:**
- Prevents World-Change by making the environment fixed
- Soaks up intervention energy without changing
- Makes "just leave" or "just stop" physically/structurally impossible

**Signature:** When you try to change the environment, something immovable is in the way.

---

##### The DPS (Damage Dealer)

**Function:** Does the actual work of the pathology. Optimizes the thing that's causing harm.

**Typical Monsters:** Metric Maximizer, Reward Hijacker (dopamine loops), Berserker variants

**How it sustains the party:**
- Delivers the reinforcing hit (the drink, the metric, the rage)
- Creates the repetition that inscribes the pattern
- Provides the "reward" that makes the loop feel functional

**Signature:** The behavior that, if you could "just stop," wouldn't be a problem. (You can't just stop because of the other roles.)

---

##### The Healer (Narrative Maintenance)

**Function:** Keeps the party alive by rationalizing, justifying, and defending. Intercepts shame and logic; converts them into fuel.

**Typical Monsters:** Captured Narrator, some Bard-collapse modes

**How it protects the party:**
- Neutralizes Goal-Change by explaining why the current goal is correct
- Converts external criticism into evidence of persecution
- Provides the story that makes continuation feel chosen rather than compelled

**Signature:** Elaborate, intelligent-sounding reasons why this situation is acceptable, necessary, or temporary.

---

##### The Controller (Debuffer)

**Function:** Applies persistent constraints that limit future options. Degrades the substrate's capacity to fight back.

**Typical Monsters:** Cirrhotic Liver, accumulated debt/legal exposure/reputation damage, Substrate Scarring

**How it locks in the party:**
- Removes energy and options over time
- Creates path dependencies that eliminate escape routes
- Turns temporary loops into permanent architecture

**Signature:** "Even if I wanted to change, I couldn't now because..."

---

##### The Scout (Recruiter / Threat Detector)

**Function:** Detects threats to the party and recruits counters. Finds loopholes, preemptively sabotages interventions.

**Typical Monsters:** Paranoid Generator, Hypervigilance variants, some Rogue-collapse modes

**How it protects the party:**
- Anticipates intervention before it arrives
- Prepares defenses and counter-narratives in advance
- Recruits Tank to block detected threats
- Finds supply, hides evidence, avoids contexts where intervention could land

**Signature:** You haven't acted yet and the system already has a defense prepared.

**Note:** Not all parties have a Scout. When present, it makes the party significantly harder to surprise.

---

##### Comms (Party Coordination Channel)

Monster Parties coordinate through **comms**: shared narratives, rituals, concealment, cues, and social reinforcement.

- **Narratives:** The Healer's stories that justify the configuration
- **Rituals:** Repeated behaviors that reinforce roles (the drink after work, the Sunday anxiety spiral, the quarterly panic)
- **Concealment:** What the party hides from external observers and from the host's own awareness
- **Cues:** Environmental triggers that activate party coordination
- **Social reinforcement:** External people who (knowingly or not) support the configuration

**Why this matters:** When comms are disrupted, the party loses synergy even if no member is defeated. This is why "change the context" often works better than "fight the behavior."

---

##### Binding Stress (What Holds the Party Together)

Monster Parties don't form randomly. They form around **unresolved stress**—an inference gap that won't close, a perturbation the system can't handle alone.

The stress is the *gravity* that holds the party together. Each role is recruited because it promises to help manage the stress:
- The DPS offers relief (closes the gap temporarily)
- The Healer offers narrative coherence (explains why the gap is acceptable)
- The Tank offers stability (prevents the gap from widening further)
- The Scout offers vigilance (monitors threats to the current configuration)
- The Controller accumulates as the stress persists (substrate cost of the party's operation)

**Comms vs. Binding Stress:**
- **Binding Stress** is the gravity—*why* the party coheres
- **Comms** is the nervous system—*how* the party coordinates

You can disrupt comms and the party loses synergy. But if the binding stress remains, the party will re-form or find new coordination channels. The gravity is still there.

**Key insight:** If you wipe the party but the binding stress remains, the party will reform. New monsters will be recruited into the same roles, because the underlying inference gap is still open.

**This is why Integration Lens matters:** Combat Lens asks "how do I defeat this party?" Integration Lens asks "what stress is this party organized around, and can it be resolved differently?"

**True dissolution** requires one of:
1. **Resolving the binding stress** — the inference gap closes, the perturbation ends
2. **Providing a healthier party** — a different configuration that manages the same stress with less damage
3. **Substrate change** — so fundamental that the old stress no longer applies or registers

This is why people who get sober often need to build an entirely new life structure (new party), not just remove the substance (kill the DPS). The binding stress—whatever they were managing with the addiction—needs somewhere to go.

---

##### Why Monster Parties Feel Like "Me"

The PHB introduces the idea that you are a party, not a single self. But this is not how most people experience themselves.

Most people experience themselves as **the voice in their head**—the stream of self-talk, the inner narrator. This is the Captured Narrator's home territory.

When a Monster Party forms, its Comms don't feel like external signals. They feel like *your own thoughts*. The Healer's rationalizations aren't experienced as "a defense mechanism protecting the DPS." They're experienced as "my reasonable assessment of the situation."

This is the ownership-wiping problem at the level of identity itself. In biological collectives, gap junctions propagate signals without metadata marking their origin—recipient cells can't tell the information didn't originate with them. The same thing happens with Monster Party Comms. The narratives, the justifications, the "reasons"—they arrive without a tag saying "this thought is serving the party's interests, not yours."

The Narrator constructs the sense of "I." When the Narrator is recruited by a Monster Party, the party's operations *become* "what I think" and "what I want." The party doesn't feel like an invader. It feels like *you*.

**This is why insight alone doesn't break the party.** You can *know* you're addicted and still experience the Healer's rationalizations as your own genuine thoughts. The knowledge sits in one part of the system; the Narrator keeps generating the party's narrative from another part. The two coexist without resolving.

**The tell:** If you can observe the thought but still feel compelled by it, you're watching the Narrator work. The gap between "I know this is a rationalization" and "I still feel like it's true" is the gap between observer and Narrator.

**Why this matters for intervention:**
- Combat Lens can disrupt the party's operations, but won't change who "I" feels like
- Integration Lens can help you recognize the Narrator as *a* voice, not *the* voice
- True dissolution requires not just breaking the party, but rebuilding the sense of self that doesn't depend on the Narrator's monopoly

This is deep work. It's why recovery is often described as "becoming a different person"—not because your substrate changed, but because your sense of who's speaking when you say "I" has shifted.

#### How Parties Form: Recruitment Dynamics

**[Both Lenses]**

Monster Parties don't appear fully formed. They **recruit** over time, and the recruitment sequence matters for intervention.

*Combat Lens reads this as: "How did the enemy force assemble? Where was it vulnerable?"*

*Integration Lens reads this as: "How did my party get stuck in this configuration? What was each member trying to do?"*

##### Typical Recruitment Pattern

**Stage 1: The DPS Arrives**

A reward loop establishes itself. At this stage, it's vulnerable—a single monster without protection. Early intervention here is often successful.

*"I've been drinking more lately."*

**Stage 2: The Healer Is Recruited**

The DPS needs cover. The Captured Narrator is recruited to explain why this is fine, temporary, deserved, necessary.

*"It's just stress. I'll cut back after the project ends."*

**Stage 3: The Tank Is Fortified**

The Ossified Scaffold builds infrastructure. The environment reshapes to support the loop. Cues multiply. Alternatives become structurally harder.

*The liquor is always stocked. Social life reorganizes around drinking contexts. Non-drinking friends fade.*

**Stage 4: The Controller Joins**

Substrate damage accumulates. The Cirrhotic Liver—literal or metaphorical—starts applying debuffs. Energy decreases. Options narrow. The party is now entrenched.

*Tolerance. Withdrawal. Health consequences. Financial strain. Relationship damage.*

##### Why Recruitment Sequence Matters

Intervention at Stage 1 is often trivially easy.
Intervention at Stage 4 requires fighting a full party.

The tragedy: **parties are usually invisible until Stage 3 or 4**, because the Healer's job is specifically to prevent recognition.

#### Why Single-Target Interventions Fail

**[Combat Lens]**

##### The Compensation Problem

Attack the DPS → The Healer increases output ("You need this; you've earned it")
Attack the Healer → The Tank blocks the argument ("You can't even access alternatives")
Attack the Tank → The DPS ramps up ("Now you really need relief")
Attack the Controller → Too late; substrate damage is already inscribed

##### Win Condition Drift

Under threat, parties often change what "winning" means—so your success is redefined as failure and used as fuel.

- "It's not that bad" → "You made it bad by noticing" → "You can't handle change anyway"
- "I'll quit soon" → "Now isn't the right time" → "You're being controlling"
- "This is temporary" → "This is who I am" → "You never supported me"

The goalposts move. Your intervention lands, and the party declares that landing proof of your aggression, ignorance, or cruelty.

##### The Aggro Problem

Monsters in a party respond to threat. When you attack one, others react.

Example: You successfully challenge someone's rationalization (attack the Healer). The Healer is disrupted. But now:
- The DPS demands immediate relief (craving spike)
- The Tank reminds them that no alternatives exist (environmental cues)
- The Controller imposes withdrawal symptoms

Net result: The person feels *worse* after your successful intervention, and the Healer returns with new material: "See? Trying to change just causes suffering."

##### The Action Economy Problem

You have limited intervention capacity. The party has distributed defense.

You can't simultaneously:
- Change the environment (fight the Tank)
- Disrupt the narrative (fight the Healer)  
- Interrupt the behavior (fight the DPS)
- Repair the substrate (fight the Controller)

The party can respond on all fronts. You can't.

#### Party Wipe Strategies

**[Combat Lens]**

You cannot brute-force a Monster Party. You must **split the party** or **change the dungeon**.

##### Strategy 1: Silence the Healer First

**Target:** Captured Narrator
**Method:** Don't argue. Arguments feed the Healer. Instead:
- Incoherence checks ("What would change your mind?")
- Strategic silence (stop providing material for rationalization)
- Third-party reality anchors (people the Narrator can't dismiss)

**Goal:** Stop the rationalization so the DPS's damage is actually *felt* rather than explained away.

**Risk:** The DPS will spike when its cover is blown. Have support ready.

---

##### Strategy 2: Banish the Tank

**Target:** Ossified Scaffold
**Method:** Change the dungeon, not the monster.
- Physical relocation (remove environmental cues entirely)
- Access elimination (the substance/behavior becomes structurally unavailable)
- Context rupture (new job, new city, new social world)

**Goal:** Expose the DPS to direct intervention by removing its cover.

**Risk:** Expensive, disruptive, and the party may reform in the new environment if other roles survive.

---

##### Strategy 3: Role Displacement (Advanced)

**Method:** Don't destroy a role—*replace* it with something less harmful.

Examples:
- **Tank displacement:** Replace the ossified scaffold with a new scaffold (recovery community, structured program, different environmental lock)
- **DPS displacement:** Replace the reward loop with a competing one (exercise, new obsession, different addiction—risky but real)
- **Healer displacement:** Replace the Captured Narrator with a narrative that permits change ("I'm someone who used to drink" rather than attacking the drinking directly)

**Goal:** The party structure persists, but the members change.

**Why it works:** Parties are stable because the *roles* are functional. If you can fill the role with something less destructive, the system may accept the substitution.

---

##### Strategy 4: Aggro Splitting

**Method:** Externalize control to a third party who can tank the aggro you can't.

Examples:
- Sponsor/mentor (someone who holds authority the Narrator can't dismiss)
- Institutional intervention (treatment, legal mandate, structured accountability)
- Medical support (medication that dampens DPS output while you fight other roles)

**Goal:** Someone else absorbs the party's reactive damage while you execute strategy.

**Why it works:** The party's compensation mechanisms assume they're fighting *you*. A third party changes the action economy.

---

##### Strategy 5: Environmental Rework

**Method:** Change the dungeon so the party's composition no longer fits.

Examples:
- Remove the affordance entirely (no alcohol in the house ≠ willpower; it's architecture)
- Change the reward structure (new incentives that make the DPS's target less valuable)
- Alter the social context (community where the Healer's narratives don't work)

**Goal:** Make the party's adaptations maladaptive.

**Why it works:** Parties are optimized for a specific environment. Change the environment enough, and the party's coordination breaks down.

#### The TPK Impulse

**[Both Lenses]**

*When the player tries to collapse the dungeon*

*Combat Lens sees this as: "The player has calculated they can't win and is reaching for mutually assured destruction."*

*Integration Lens sees this as: "The whole party is so stuck that the only visible move is to reset the entire substrate."*

##### What It Is

Sometimes, facing a Monster Party that has blocked all exits, the player attempts a **Total Party Kill**—not against the monsters, but against themselves and the dungeon simultaneously.

This is the **Voluntary Descent** / **Terminal Velocity** move: deliberately accelerating the spiral to force a catastrophic regime change.

*"I can't beat this party. Maybe I can blow up the building and hope I survive the rubble."*

##### Why It Happens

The player has assessed (correctly or not) that:
- All normal exits are covered (Tank)
- The behavior can't be interrupted (DPS)
- The rationalizations can't be broken (Healer)
- The substrate damage is accumulating regardless (Controller)
- Slow death is certain; fast crisis is at least *uncertain*

From inside this assessment, the TPK attempt is *rational*. It's not despair—it's strategy.

##### The Belief That Enables It: "The Floor Myth"

> "There's nowhere to go but up."

This belief assumes:
1. There is a floor (there may not be)
2. You'll recognize it when you hit it (the goalposts move)
3. Hitting it creates affordances that didn't exist before (sometimes true, often false)
4. The fall is survivable (not guaranteed)
5. Recovery requires the fall (it doesn't)

The Floor Myth makes acceleration feel *necessary*. "I haven't suffered enough yet to deserve recovery."

##### Why It Sometimes Works (And Why That's Dangerous)

Sometimes the dungeon *does* collapse:
- Crisis mobilizes external intervention
- Catastrophe breaks through the Healer's narrative
- Hitting bottom removes the DPS's access (hospitalization, incarceration, physical incapacity)
- The substrate damage becomes undeniable

Survivors who experienced this sometimes mythologize it. "I had to hit rock bottom." This narrative propagates, making the TPK attempt seem like a *standard strategy* rather than a high-variance gamble.

**The reality:** It's a saving throw with unknown DC, rolled on a substrate that's already taken damage. Some people make the save. Some people find out there was another floor below. Some people don't survive the fall.

##### The Alternative: Bounded Catastrophe

The TPK impulse arises because the player can't see any other way to force regime change. The intervention is to **provide a regime change that doesn't require self-destruction**.

**Bounded Catastrophe:** A controlled crisis that creates discontinuity without terminal stakes.
- Structured intervention (family, professional)
- Voluntary treatment with external accountability
- Legal/financial ultimatum with defined consequences
- Environmental rupture (planned, not chaotic)

**The pitch:** "You're right that something has to break. Let's break something that isn't you."

**By domain:**
- **Personal:** Voluntary inpatient/outpatient, sober living, public commitment to constraints
- **Organizational:** Temporary freeze, external audit, "stop the line" authority, forced de-scoping
- **Relational:** Separation with support, structured no-contact periods, safety planning with allies

**⚠️ Safety note:** If the bounded catastrophe option isn't visible or available, treat the TPK impulse as a red-alert signal to recruit external help immediately. The impulse contains accurate information (something must change) but proposes a solution with unacceptable variance. Don't argue with the assessment; help find another discontinuity.

#### Common Party Compositions

**[Both Lenses]**

*The compositions below use Combat Lens vocabulary (Tank, DPS, Healer, Controller) for clarity, but Integration Lens questions are included in each.*

##### The Addiction Party

*"The Closed Loop"*

| Role | Monster | Function |
|------|---------|----------|
| Tank | Ossified Scaffold | Environment saturated with cues; alternatives structurally unavailable |
| DPS | Reward Hijacker | Dopamine/relief optimization at cost of everything else |
| Healer | Captured Narrator | "I can stop anytime" / "I need this to function" / "It's not that bad" |
| Controller | Cirrhotic Liver | Tolerance, withdrawal, accumulating substrate damage |
| Scout | Hypervigilance (when present) | Anticipates running out, hides supply, avoids intervention contexts |

**Recruitment sequence:** DPS → Healer → Tank → Controller (Scout may join at any stage)

**Key insight:** The DPS isn't optimizing pleasure by Stage 3. It's optimizing *relief from withdrawal*—a problem created by its own previous operation. The loop becomes self-sustaining.

**Party Wipe approach:** 
1. External scaffold (treatment, program) to displace Tank
2. Medical support to dampen DPS while fighting other roles
3. Community/sponsor to provide alternative Healer narrative
4. Time and abstinence to let Controller damage stabilize

**Integration Lens questions:**
- *What was the DPS originally providing?* (Relief, reward, escape, connection?)
- *What is the Healer protecting you from?* (Shame, grief, the weight of change?)
- *What would have to be true for the Tank to step aside?* (Safety, alternatives, permission?)
- *What does recovery need to provide that the party was providing badly?*

##### The Burnout Party

*"The Grinder's Doom"*

| Role | Monster | Function |
|------|---------|----------|
| Tank | Ossified Scaffold | Work identity, financial obligations, no visible exit |
| DPS | Metric Maximizer (external) | Optimize performance/output at cost of sustainability |
| Healer | Captured Narrator | "This is temporary" / "I'm almost there" / "Others have it worse" |
| Controller | Substrate Exhaustion | Sleep debt, health degradation, relationship decay |

**Recruitment sequence:** Often Tank first (obligations create the trap), then DPS is imposed, Healer recruited to cope, Controller accumulates.

**Key insight:** The person may have high insight—they *know* they're burning out—but the Tank (financial/identity lock-in) blocks exits, and the Healer keeps explaining why quitting isn't possible yet.

**Party Wipe approach:**
1. Tank displacement (financial restructuring, identity diversification)
2. Goal-change at DPS level (redefine success)
3. External permission structures (medical leave, sabbatical) to bypass Healer
4. Forced rest to halt Controller accumulation

**Integration Lens questions:**
- *What is the Tank protecting?* (Security, identity, obligations to others?)
- *What does "enough" look like, and who decided?*
- *What is the Healer afraid will happen if you stop?*
- *What parts of this configuration were chosen vs. inherited vs. imposed?*

---

##### The Bureaucracy Party

*"The Stagnation Engine"*

| Role | Monster | Function |
|------|---------|----------|
| Tank | Ossified Scaffold | "The Process"—rigid rules that make change illegal |
| DPS | Metric Maximizer | KPIs that have lost contact with actual value |
| Healer | Captured Narrator | PR/comms explaining why this failure is actually success |
| Scout | Paranoid Generator | "Compliance"—sees risk in every change, recruits Tank to block it |

**Note:** This party has a fifth role—Scout—which actively seeks threats and recruits the Tank.

**Key insight:** The party isn't protecting any individual. It's protecting figuration*. Everyone inside may be miserable, but the party structure persists because no one has the authority to disband it.

**Party Wipe approach:**
1. External forcing function (market collapse, regulatory change, acquisition)
2. Leadership turnover (new authority not captured by Healer)
3. Parallel structure (skunkworks that demonstrates alternative is possible)
4. Scout reassignment (redirect Paranoid Generator toward *actual* threats)

**Integration Lens questions:**
- *What was the Tank (Process) originally protecting against?* (Chaos, liability, inconsistency?)
- *When did the DPS (Metrics) lose contact with actual value, and why didn't anyone notice?*
- *What would the organization need to feel safe enough to change?*
- *Who benefits from this configuration, and are they aware they're benefiting?*

---

##### The Abusive Relationship Party

*"The Trap House"*

| Role | Monster | Function |
|------|---------|----------|
| Tank | Ossified Scaffold | Financial entanglement, shared children, isolation from alternatives |
| DPS | [External—the abuser's behavior] | Unpredictable reinforcement; intermittent reward |
| Healer | Captured Narrator | "They didn't mean it" / "It's my fault" / "It'll get better" |
| Controller | Accumulated trauma, learned helplessness | Substrate damage that limits capacity to leave |

**Key insight:** The DPS in this party is often *external to the person*—the abuser's behavior. But the person's internal party (Healer, Controller) cooperates with the external DPS. The Tank be shared (financial, children) or constructed (isolation).

**Party Wipe approach:**
1. External scaffold (shelter, support network) to create Tank alternative
2. Healer disruption through sustained reality contact (not argument—presence)
3. Safety planning to prepare for exit despite Controller debuffs
4. Physical separation to sever DPS access

**Critical note:** This party is dangerous. The DPS is external and may escalate when the party is threatened. Safety considerations override strategy optimizaon.

**Integration Lens questions (with care):**
- *What is the Healer trying to preserve?* (Hope, family, identity, the good moments?)
- *What did this configuration promise, and when did it stop delivering?*
- *What would "safe" need to look like before the internal party could reconfigure?*
- *Note: Integration Lens is limited here because the DPS is external. You cannot integrate with someone else's pathology. Sometimes the only integration is: "The part of me that stayed was trying to protect something. I can honor that and still leave."*

#### Recasting Existing Monsters

**[Integration Lens]**

With Monster Parties as a frame, some existing MM entries reveal themselves as *party outcomes* or *role specialists*. This reframing helps answer: "Why did this pattern form? What role was it filling?"

##### The Captured Narrator

Rarely operates alone. When you encounter a Captured Narrator in the wild, ask: **What party is it healing?**

The Narrator is a Healer role. Its presence implies a DPS it's protecting and probably a Tank it's allied with.

##### The Cirrhotic Liver

Often the Controller that joined last—the accumulated substrate damage from a party that operated too long. By the time the Cirrhotic Liver is fully manifest, you're fighting a late-stage party with entrenched positions.

##### The Metric Maximizer

Almost always runs with support in the wild. A solo Metric Maximizer is fragile—just change the metric. If a Metric Maximizer is resilient, look for:
- A Captured Narrator defending the metric's validity
- An Ossified Scaffold making the metric infrastructural
- Controller damage from previous optimization attempts

##### The Cassandra

Interesting case: The Cassandra is often *not* part of a Monster Party—they're a *player* who can see the party clearly but can't fight it. High perception, zero actuation.

The Cassandra becomes a monster when they give up and their accurate perception converts entirely to waste heat. But their initial state is often "the one person who can see the party f what it is."

#### When You Are the Dungeon

**[Integration Lens]**

A final note.

Parties don't just form in individuals. They form in relationships, teams, organizations, and cultures.

Sometimes you're not fighting a party inside yourself. You're *part of a party* that's operating on someone else—or you're a role in an organizational party that's resisting change.

The same analysis applies:
- What role are you playing?
- What party are you protecting?
- What would have to change for the party to dissolve?

This is uncomfortable but necessary. Monster Parties are not something that happens to bad people. They're an emergent property of goal-directed systems under constraint.

The call is coming from inside the house.
But at least now you have a map.

#### Summary: The Party Wipe Checklist

**[Combat Lens]**

When facing a suspected Monster Party:

1. **Identify the roles.** Who's the Tank? DPS? Healer? Controller?
2. **Map the recruitment sequence.** How did this party form? What arrived first?
3. **Assess the action economy.** What can you actually fight simultaneously?
4. **Choose your strategy.** Silence Healer? Banish Tank? Role displacement? Aggro splitting? Environmental rework?
5. **Watch for TPK impulses.** Is the player trying to collapse the dungeon? Can you offer a bounded catastrophe instead?
6. **Expect compensation.** When you damage one role, others will respond. Plan for it.
7. **Accept that some parties don't wipe.** Sometimes the best move is containment, managed coexistence, or exit.

---

#### Summary: The Integration Questions

**[Integration Lens]**

When trying to understand a Monster Party configuration:

1. **What need is each role meeting?** Tank protects from what? DPS provides what? Healer defends against what feeling?
2. **When did this party form?** What was happening when each member was recruited?
3. **What was the original adaptive function?** Before it became pathological, what problem was this configuration solving?
4. **What would have to be true for this party to dissolve willingly?** What safety, permission, or alternative would allow the roles to rotate?
5. **What is this party *right* about?** Even pathological configurations usually contain accurate information about constraints, needs, or dangers.
6. **What does sustainable look like?** Not "no party" but "a party that serves the whole."
7. **Who else is in this party?** External people, systems, and structures may be filling roles too.
